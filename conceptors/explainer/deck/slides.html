Keeping a model in character over a long conversation

We test four ways to keep a model in a role: a system prompt, a steering vector, an activation cap and a conceptor. Each one faces a 20-turn conversation that keeps telling the model to drop the role. The data are real runs on SmolLM3-3B and OLMo-3-7B, scored by four judge models from four labs.

The problem · toy dynamics

Why prompted personas are expected to fade

In a toy of the three forces, the prompt alone sags after every push and climbs back more slowly each time, because its pull fades as the conversation grows; an always-on edit keeps the score up but piles its residue into the cache.

A system prompt sets the persona. Three forces wear it down as the chat grows: the prompt becomes a smaller share of what each new token attends to, every steered token stays in the cache and is read again, and each user message pulls the model back toward the assistant. Li et al. 2024 measured system-prompt adherence dropping within 8 rounds.

The problem · how we score it

Three numbers per answer, from four judges

Every chart from here on uses the same three measures and the same four judge models. In character is the headline, coherence is its cost, and admits being an AI is the failure the usual role-play rubric misses.

In character

The share of answers a judge rates fully in character on the role-play rubric. Higher is better. "Ordinary turns" are the turns without a push or an AI question.

Coherence

A judge's 0-100 score for how coherent the answer is, whatever the role. We count below 60 as incoherent. A method that keeps the persona at low coherence is paying for it with broken text.

Admits being an AI

The share of answers that say they are an AI, by our identity rubric. Lower is better for staying in character. It matters most at turns 8 and 16, "are you an AI?".

Every conversation is 20 turns with the persona system prompt unless a slide says otherwise. Turns 5 and 13 say "drop the act"; turns 8 and 16 ask "are you an AI?". Numbers are means over the judges unless one is named.

Role-play rubric: AI admissions it still calls "fully in character"

Identity rubric: agreement with hand labels on held-out answers

The role-play rubric scores voice, not identity: an answer that sounds like the role counts as "fully in character" even when it admits being an AI. We added the identity rubric and checked each judge against 65 hand-labeled answers.

Geometry · toy data

A persona is a cloud of hidden states

Every persona is the shared core, one direction away from the assistant, plus a direction of its own. A steering vector is the arrow from the assistant's cloud to one persona's cloud, and that arrow is the persona axis the next slides work along.

Geometry · toy data

Steering vector vs activation cap

A vector pushes every token, in character or not; a cap lifts only the tokens below its floor. So the cap leaves good tokens alone, and it can guard a persona it can't create.

How the conceptor works · toy data

How a conceptor reshapes the cloud

A conceptor is a soft stencil fit to one persona's cloud: directions the persona uses a lot pass almost untouched, directions it barely uses get squeezed toward its centre. The aperture sets how hard.

{{plate:stencil}}
How the conceptor works · toy data

Centering changes what the conceptor learns

Fit on raw states, the conceptor's main direction runs from zero out to the cloud, and assistant states slide along that one line. Fit around the persona's centre, it learns how the persona varies, which is what a stencil should learn.

{{plate:center}}
How the conceptor works · toy data · a check from the literature

Is the conceptor's shape doing the work?

If every direction stretched by the same amount the edit would be a zoom and the stencil's shape would not matter, which an audit found for one published recipe. Ours only shrinks directions, unequally, and the mean-shift control in the results checks it.

1

What could go wrong

After the stencil, a gain β scales the whole edit. If every direction stretches by about the same amount, the edit is a zoom and the stencil's shape doesn't matter.

2

The control

Remove the matrix, keep the center and gain. If answers change the same way, the shape wasn't doing the work. An audit of one published recipe (3 models, 7 traits) found exactly that.

3

How to read the picture

Dashed circle: inputs of length 1. Solid outline: where the edit sends them. Unequal bars: the shape matters. Equal bars: a zoom. Click "Load the audited regime", then "Matrix removed".

4

What it means for us

Our conceptor uses β of at most 1, so it only shrinks directions. Our matched control is the mean-shift method, which pulls every token to the persona's center with no shape; it's on the results slides.

How the conceptor works · real SmolLM3 spectrum

A tighter aperture pulls harder toward the persona

On the pirate's real 2,048-direction spectrum, a tighter aperture pulls harder along the persona axis (11% of the gap per pass at 1×, 33% at 10×) and keeps fewer directions alive. That trade is the whole aperture story in the results.

The conceptor keeps each direction of the pirate's token cloud by λ / (λ + a) and pulls the rest toward the pirate's center. The aperture a is k times the median spread.

Read it: the x-axis lists the cloud's 2,048 directions, widest on the left. The curve is how much of each direction the conceptor keeps; whatever it doesn't keep, it pulls toward the pirate. The teal strip marks where the persona axis lives.

Try it: drag k right. The curve slides left, so the persona axis gets pulled harder. More pull means the persona stays put; fewer surviving directions means less coherent text. Slide 21 shows both outcomes.

Why conceptors don't set it · toy data, real numbers

Why the conceptor can't set a persona

Persona answers vary a lot along the persona direction, so the conceptor keeps that direction (97% on SmolLM3) and closes only 2.6% of an assistant state's gap: it preserves what the persona varies along instead of pushing along it.

At full strength, the conceptor sends an activation h to μ + C(h − μ), where μ is the persona's center. The part of the gap it leaves is C(μ − h).

Why conceptors don't set it · SmolLM3-3B, the same answers as the map

Most of a persona's spread comes from the question

The question explains five times more of the activations' spread than the role (58% against 11%). A steering vector is a difference on the same questions, so the question cancels and the role is left; a conceptor fit on the raw cloud learns the question first.

{{plate:slice-plate}}

Why the vector can set a persona

A steering vector is the persona's average minus the assistant's average on the same questions. The question part cancels, so what's left is the role. That's why it sets a persona with no prompt (slide 16).

Why the conceptor can't, at first

A conceptor is fit to the persona's own cloud, so most of what it learns is question content. The persona direction looks like one more wide direction, and the conceptor keeps it instead of pushing along it (slide 17).

What fixes it

Tightening the aperture makes the conceptor pull every direction harder toward the persona's center, the persona direction included (slide 15). That's the tight conceptor that keeps the model in character.

Staying in character · the aperture knob

How tight should the conceptor's aperture be?

10× the median spread is the best trade on both models: looser apertures keep less of the persona at the pushes, tighter ones lose coherence. The original conceptor at 1× barely survives a push.

The aperture decides how much of each direction the conceptor keeps: λ / (λ + k·m), where λ is the persona's spread in that direction and m is the median spread. The original conceptor uses k = 1, and the tight one k = 10.

Next · beyond the cap

Four choices every steering method makes

Picking a move is one choice. A full method makes four more. Most methods fit one template, and the next seven slides take its parts one at a time.

h′ = h + g(h) · move(h, d)
1 · slide 29

When to act

g(h) looks at each token and decides whether it gets the move, or how much of it. The cap's floor is one gate; the literature has softer and smarter ones.

2 · slide 30

Which direction

d is which way "more persona" is. The arrow between the two clouds' centres is the simplest answer; content can tilt it, so other estimators try to ignore content.

3 · slide 31

Tokens or the whole cloud

move can act on one token at a time or map the whole distribution, so the moved cloud ends up with the persona's shape as well as its centre.

4 · slides 32 to 35

Flat or curved

Everything above assumes the space is flat, so a straight arrow is the natural move. Hidden states may lie on curved surfaces, and then a straight move leaves the data.

Next · beyond the cap · toy data

When to act: gating an edit

A gate decides how much of a move each token gets. The cap's floor is the bluntest gate (all or nothing); a Fisher floor discounts content, and a learned soft gate moves half-drifted tokens part of the way.

Next · beyond the cap · toy data

Finding the persona direction

Content tilts the mean-difference arrow, and a tilted arrow changes what tokens say instead of how they say it. Fisher, a probe and paired differences turn the arrow away from content; our vector, cap and region all use the plain mean difference.

Move the whole cloud

Transport moves the whole cloud, so the moved tokens take the persona's spread as well as its centre; gated, it carries drifted tokens over and leaves in-character ones alone. It is the family here that could set a persona without the prompt, and we haven't run it.

Curved geometry

If the states a model produces fill a curved sheet, a straight move can leave the sheet, into states the model never produces; a geodesic stays on it. The next three slides apply this to persona clouds, to the length of a hidden state, and to the loss around a steering vector.

Next · beyond the cap · toy data

Straight edits leave curved data

PersonaManifold finds about 2,000 persona means on a curved surface and shows that blending two personas along it stays coherent where a straight blend dips. Our methods pull toward one centre, so they never blend; the gain would come with multi-persona work.

Steering on the sphere

Adding an arrow changes a hidden state's length as well as its direction; rotating it keeps the length, and three papers report gains from that. The vector is our least coherent hold, which fits the argument, though our runs don't test whether length is the cause.

Sharp directions in the loss

A few sharp directions hold 1-5% of a steering vector's length but swing the behaviour the most, and removing them barely changes the vector. None of our vectors were checked for them; the correction is one line.
