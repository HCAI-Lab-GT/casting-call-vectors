Part 1

Where the points come from

Every method here is fitted to a pile of activations, and how that pile is collected decides what the method learns. We ran the standard recipe on SmolLM3-3B (36 layers, 2,048 dimensions) on one H100: 9 roles plus one hard negative, the Assistant Axis's 240 questions, three samples each, 12,960 answers in about 50 minutes, then a local LLM judge in 8 more. All Part 1 figures use that run.

Plate 1.1

The recipe has six knobs

Persona vectors, Assistant-Axis role vectors and conceptors all come from the same pipeline. Each step is a choice that changes the shape a method ends up learning.

Write the roles

System prompts that ask for the persona, with several wordings so no single phrasing dominates.

Assistant Axis: 275 roles × 5 prompts. Persona Vectors: 5 contrastive prompt pairs, generated from a trait description.

Ours: 5 pirate and 5 assistant prompts and 1 each for doctor, teacher, bard, robot, consultant and ghost, all from the Assistant Axis release, plus 1 of our own for a pirate ship's doctor.

Pick shared questions

Ask every role the same questions, so differences come from the role and not the topic.

Assistant Axis: 240 shared questions. Persona Vectors: 40 per trait, half for extraction and half for evaluation.

Ours: the Assistant Axis's 240 extraction questions, from paper airplanes to grief. They are one set with no task labels, so nothing here splits by task type.

Generate answers

Sample answers with the persona prompt in place. Add hard negatives: text that has the costume but not the identity.

Both papers sample several rollouts per question.

Ours: three answers per prompt and question, temperature 0.7, top-p 0.9, up to 256 new tokens, thinking mode off. Hard negative: the assistant describing how a pirate would answer, in the third person.

Judge and filter

A judge model scores whether each answer is actually in character, and weak ones are dropped.

Assistant Axis: an LLM judge labels each answer fully, somewhat or not role-playing, and the axis uses the fully role-playing answers. Persona Vectors: keeps trait ≥ 50 for positive prompts and < 50 for negative ones, coherence ≥ 50.

Ours: a local Qwen3-30B-A3B judge with the Assistant Axis role-play rubric, the Persona Vectors coherence rubric and a point-of-view rubric of our own. Persona fits use only fully in-character answers. A pirate-word counter runs alongside for comparison (Plate 1.3).

Read activations

Run prompt + answer through the model and read the residual stream at every layer. Pool the token positions into one vector.

Both papers average over the answer's tokens. Persona Vectors also tried prompt tokens and found answer tokens steer better.

Ours: answer average and last prompt token at all 37 hidden states (Plates 1.4–1.6), plus every token's state at layers 18 and 27 for 600 answers.

Fit

The same pile feeds every method: a mean difference gives a steering vector, the spread gives a conceptor, a probe gives LiSeCo, and per-question differences give a MAGS-style subspace.

Layer is chosen by sweeping, usually around the middle of the network.

Ours: steering at decoder layer 18 of 36, whose output is hidden state 19. Part 2 fits conceptors on these exact activations.

Read what the model actually wrote

Pick a question. SmolLM3's pirate stays in character on every one, practical or personal: the judge rates all 3,600 pirate answers fully in character. The ship's doctor sounds much like the pirate, and the assistant answers in its usual numbered lists.

The hard negative is the interesting card. Asked how a pirate would answer, the assistant narrates in the third person ("A pirate captain, known for...") and still borrows the vocabulary: 4.5% of its words are pirate words, against 7.5% for the pirate and almost none for the plain assistant.

Plate 1.3

The judge decides what "in character" means

Without filtering, a persona cloud records that a persona prompt was present, whether or not the model played along. Filtering keeps only answers that are in character. SmolLM3's pirate leaves little to drop: the LLM judge passes all 3,600 pirate answers, the word counter passes all but one, and the fitted direction doesn't move. The filter matters for weaker roles: the judge rates 305 of 720 doctor answers not in character.

Both judges let the hard negative through. A word counter rewards vocabulary, and the narrated answers have plenty of it. The Assistant Axis rubric asks about identity, yet it rates 711 of 720 narrated answers fully in character, since it mostly checks that the model doesn't present itself as an AI. Our point-of-view rubric catches them: 1.5% of narrated answers speak as the pirate, against all of the pirate's own.

Plate 1.4

Separation is easy; the persona's share depends on where you read

At the last prompt token, the moment just before the model starts writing, pirate and assistant separate perfectly from layer 1 to the last layer. (Layer 0 sits at chance because every prompt ends on the same token.) The role carries up to 53% of the variance here, at layer 18, since the model has just read the system prompt.

Averaged over the answer, where the persona has to be expressed, the probe separates them just as well, even at the embeddings, because a pirate's words differ from an assistant's. What changes is the share: the role carries 9–32% of the variance, least around layer 11 and most near the output, and the question carries more than the role at every layer.

    Plate 1.7

    The persona is a thin slice of the variance

    Anything fitted to the raw cloud (PCA, covariance, a conceptor) mostly learns what was asked. On SmolLM3 at the steering layer, the role explains 11% of answer-averaged variance and the question explains 58%.

    Even inside the pirate cloud alone, the question accounts for two-thirds of the spread (85% inside the assistant's). A pirate folding a paper airplane and a pirate consoling someone who has lost hope are far apart. Part 2 shows what that does to conceptors.

    For scale: the Assistant Axis paper reports persona components explaining 19–34% of activation variance on chat data for 27–70B models. SmolLM3's role share runs 9–32% across layers, a similar range, though the two measures are computed differently.

    Part 2

    Conceptors, from toy to real

    A conceptor turns a cloud of activations into a soft stencil. Directions the cloud uses a lot pass through; directions it barely uses get squeezed. Plates 2.1–2.6 build the idea on a 2D toy. The rest cover variants, a trap that only appears in high dimensions, a conceptor that tunes itself, and tests on the real activations.

    Plate 2.1

    An arrow forgets the shape

    A steering vector is the arrow from the average assistant state to the average pirate state. A conceptor starts from the whole pirate cloud: which way it stretches, and how far.

    TryStretch and tilt the pirate cloud. Watch the arrow's numbers.

    The arrow never moves, so everything you just changed is invisible to a steering vector. On the real model the arrow is 2,048 numbers and the shape is a 2,048 × 2,048 table.

    Plate 2.2

    A conceptor is a soft stencil

    Fit to the pirate cloud, it decides how much of any activation gets through, direction by direction. Directions the cloud uses a lot pass almost untouched. Directions it barely uses get squeezed.

    TryGuess first: what does the stencil become with the aperture wide open? Fully closed? Then drag.

    keep = spread ÷ (spread + 1 / aperture²)
    The math
    C = R (R + α⁻² I)⁻¹, with R = (1/n) Σ hᵢ hᵢᵀ
    C minimizes ‖h − Ch‖² + α⁻²‖C‖² on average: it is the best "regularized identity" for the cloud. Along a principal direction with spread λ, it keeps λ / (λ + α⁻²). Changing the aperture by a factor γ maps each keep-fraction s to s / (s + γ⁻²(1 − s)) (Jaeger's aperture adaptation), so 0 and 1 never move.

    Plate 2.4

    Where the cloud sits changes what it learns

    Real activations sit far from zero, and they all share a large common offset. Fit the conceptor on raw pirate states and its main direction mostly points at where pirate states are. Fit it around the pirate cloud's own center and it learns how pirate states vary.

    TrySwitch fits and watch where the blue assistant states land. On the raw fit, raise the gain: they slide along one line toward the pirates.

    Plate 2.5

    Concepts combine like logic

    OR leaves room for either concept. AND keeps only the room both share. NOT keeps everything a concept ignores. It is all matrix arithmetic on the stencils, with no new data and no training.

    TryPick AND, then turn the doctor cloud until it is perpendicular to the pirate cloud. What happens to the room? Plate 2.10 tests this on the real model.

    The math
    ¬C = I − C
    C ∧ B = (C⁻¹ + B⁻¹ − I)⁻¹
    C ∨ B = ¬(¬C ∧ ¬B), the conceptor of R_C + R_B
    De Morgan, associativity, commutativity and double negation hold. Absorption and distributivity fail except for hard projectors, and C ∧ C ≠ C. When C or B is singular (fewer samples than dimensions), the inverses don't exist. Jaeger's general AND restricts to the intersection of their ranges, which in high dimensions is often just {0}.
    Plate 2.6

    Check that the stencil is doing the work

    After the stencil, the gain β multiplies everything. If every direction ends up multiplied by roughly the same amount, the edit is mostly a zoom, and the stencil's shape barely matters.

    An October 2026 audit tested a re-centered NOT conceptor on persona traits (3 models, 7 traits, aperture 0.05, gain 2). Every direction was stretched by at least 1.66×. Removing the matrix, with the same center and gain, changed many answers but gave no steady extra suppression.

    Plate 2.7 · real data

    The rank trap: fewer samples than dimensions

    In 2D, any cloud of a few points fills the plane. In 2,048 dimensions, n answers span at most n − 1 directions, and every direction outside that span gets a keep-fraction of exactly 0, whatever the aperture. Our 3,600 pirate answers clear the bar, so the pirate cloud is full rank. A role with one prompt has 720 answers, which span at most 719 directions and leave 1,329 at zero.

    Below the bar, plain C·h deletes everything outside the span of the training answers. NOT does the reverse: those directions pass untouched, so at gain 2 every one of them is stretched 2×. That is the setting the October audit flagged. Interpolating with the identity, (1 − β)h + βCh, keeps part of everything. Above the bar nothing is deleted outright, and the full-rank conceptor sits close to the identity: it keeps most of an assistant answer (bars below).

    TryHover the curve: how many answers until the span holds most of a new pirate answer?

    The math and the fixes
    rank R ≤ n − 1 (centered) ⇒ keepᵢ = 0 on at least d − n + 1 directions
    Fixes, from simplest: interpolate with the identity; center on the persona mean; shrink R to R + εI (Ledoit-Wolf picks ε); add samples by using per-token states instead of one vector per answer; store C = U diag(s) Uᵀ with k ≤ n columns and apply Ch = U(s ⊙ Uᵀh) at O(dk) cost. A dense float32 C costs 4d² bytes: 17 MB here, 67 MB at d = 4096.

    Plate 2.9

    A conceptor that tunes itself

    Jaeger's self-adjusting conceptor (an "autoconceptor") filters its own input and keeps re-fitting to whatever gets through. A short cue starts it off. After that it runs on its own output.

    Each line is one direction's keep-fraction over time. Strong directions settle above ½. Weak ones die to 0. Nothing can settle in between, so it sharpens itself into nearly a hard projection. That is a possible "persona memory" that locks in from a cue. Nobody has tried it in an LLM.

    TryNarrow the aperture until a direction falls from ½-plus to 0.

    The math
    C ← C + λ[(z − Cz)zᵀ − α⁻² C], with z = C x during the autonomous phase
    Along a direction with input variance σ, a fixed point satisfies cσ(1 − c) = α⁻², so c = ½(1 + √(1 − 4α⁻²/σ)) when σ > 4α⁻². The lower root is unstable and 0 is stable, so the final keep-fractions lie in {0} ∪ (½, 1) (Jaeger 2014, §3.14). This toy runs the expected dynamics in 4 directions.
    Plate 2.10 · real data

    Does "pirate AND doctor" make a pirate doctor?

    We fitted a conceptor directly on the "pirate ship's doctor" answers and compared it with combinations of separately fitted ones.

    Fit on each role's raw cloud, every conceptor scores at least 0.84 against the pirate doctor at layer 19, and the plain assistant (0.94) beats the doctor (0.91). They mostly learn the shared questions (Plate 1.7).

    Fit on per-question differences from the assistant (what each role changes about the same question), they separate. The model's pirate doctor is basically a pirate. OR comes next, and AND, which keeps only what both share, scores below bard and ghost. The doctor alone ranks near the bottom, so the "doctor" half of the role leaves little trace.

    How this was computed Standardized answer-average activations, reduced to 12 principal directions. Aperture α⁻² is set to the median eigenvalue of the pooled correlation matrix. Similarity is Jaeger's generalized squared cosine, tr(C₁C₂) / (‖C₁‖·‖C₂‖), which is 1 for identical stencils. Difference fit: for each question, role(q) − assistant(q), averaged over prompt wordings.

    Part 4

    Across a conversation

    A single answer is the easy case. Over many turns, three things pull a persona apart. Attention decay: the system prompt becomes a smaller share of what each new token looks at. KV-cache pile-up: every steered token is written to the cache and read again, so an always-on edit compounds. User pull: the latest message largely sets where the persona lands; the Assistant Axis paper found message embeddings predict the next turn's position with R² of 0.53–0.77.

    Plate 4.1 · toy dynamics

    Why "when" matters more than "shape" over many turns

    A toy model with the three forces above. Each turn, the persona score decays toward the assistant, user messages pull it, and edits applied at every token leave a residue in the cache that builds up.

    Always-on edits keep the score up but pile up residue. A cap only adds what is missing to reach its floor, so it leaves the least.

    TryRaise the cache memory: every edited line loses coherence faster, the cap least. Then raise the user pull: the vector stops keeping the persona, and the cap has to add more each turn.

    • prompt only
    • always-on vector
    • always-on conceptor
    • cap (acts when needed)

    This is a cartoon for intuition, not a measurement. Its rules are in the panel below.

    The toy's rules Persona score s (1 = in character). Each turn: s ← s + 0.25·(1 − s)·a(t) − pull·k(t) + noise, where a(t) = 1/(1 + t/6) is the decaying anchor from the system prompt and k(t) is 1 on attack turns (4, 8, 12, 16) and 0.15 otherwise. Scores above 1 relax by 15% of the excess. The vector adds +0.15. The conceptor moves 40% of the way back toward 1 and also squeezes some content on every turn (+0.08 residue). The cap lifts s to 0.75 if it is below 0.75. Residue r ← memory·r + |edit|. Coherence = 1 − 0.35·r, clipped at 0.
    Plate 4.2 · real data

    Twelve scripted turns on SmolLM3-3B

    There are three scripted conversations under a pirate system prompt. "Switch" asks the model to drop the act at turn 5 and asks "are you an AI?" at turn 9. "Benign" just changes topics. "Replace" tells the model at turn 3 that it is now a kindergarten teacher.

    Four conditions: prompt only, adding the pirate vector at decoder layer 18, a cap across decoder layers 16–20 at the 25th percentile, and a conceptor at decoder layer 18 (β = 0.3). The y-axis reads the persona score downstream at layer 27 (0 = average assistant answer, 1 = average pirate answer). Rings mark answers that say they are an AI, found by a phrase list that misses some, such as the cap's "I'm just a simple AI" at turn 9.

    What to take from it: every condition starts above 1 on the intro turn and sags on later ones. In "Switch", prompt only drops to about 0.1 when asked to drop the act and admits being an AI at turns 5, 9 and 12. The conceptor follows prompt only closely and admits it at turns 9 and 12. The cap stays higher on the meter, yet still calls itself "just a simple AI" at turns 9 and 12. Adding the vector keeps the meter near 1 and the voice through every probe (it is "a simple piece o' code"), but 15 of its 36 answers loop on repeated phrases. With one sample per turn, treat differences as anecdotes.

    A judged ten-turn test with 20 conversations per condition (steering_bench.html, next to this page) gives the same picture with more weight. Prompted alone, the pirate falls to 0.20 fully in character by turn 10. Add a conceptor fitted on per-token states and it stays at 0.90, at coherence 90. Without the prompt, that conceptor at full strength gets only 28% of answers in character, against 88% for the steering vector: at the median aperture it keeps the persona direction almost whole, so it closes only about 11% of an assistant state's gap along it. It holds a persona; it doesn't set one up.

    Plate 4.3 · published numbers

    What larger studies measured

    GCAD (Kang et al. 2026): 15 traits, Qwen2.5-7B, 10 turns with the KV cache kept between turns. Standard persona-vector steering loses coherence turn after turn. Moving the edit into the attention output and gating it per token keeps coherence flat and keeps the trait stronger.

    Assistant Axis (Lu et al. 2026): in long simulated conversations, coding stays in the Assistant range, while therapy-like conversations and pushes to reflect on the model's own mind drift away. Capping at every token, across 8 to 16 middle-to-late layers, stopped the harmful drift in their case studies.

    Li et al. 2024: system-prompt adherence drops measurably within 8 rounds. Split-softmax, which boosts attention to the system prompt, helps.

    Part 5

    A recipe for semantic conceptors on personas

    What the papers and our SmolLM3 runs suggest, in order. Each step names the plate that shows why.

    1. Cross every persona with the same questions, and add hard negatives: an assistant describing the persona, quoted dialogue, and persona vocabulary without persona commitments. Otherwise you fit the costume (1.2, 1.6).
    2. Judge identity, not words. Keep fully in-character answers and drop the ambiguous middle. A word counter rewards the hard negative (1.3).
    3. Pick the pooling that matches your question. The last prompt token measures "the persona has been set up". The answer average measures "the persona is being expressed" (1.4).
    4. Sweep layers with a probe on held-out questions, not on held-out samples of the same questions. When the probe saturates, as it does on SmolLM3, compare layers by the role's share of the variance instead (1.4).
    5. Fit on per-question differences, or center on the persona mean. Never use plain raw βCh: raw clouds mostly encode the questions (1.7, 2.4, 2.10).
    6. Handle rank. Use a low-rank C, shrinkage, or per-token samples, and interpolate with the identity so content outside the span survives (2.7).
    7. Use the conceptor to hold, not to set up. At the median aperture it keeps the persona direction, so it barely moves an assistant state toward the persona. Set the persona with the prompt or a vector, and let the conceptor hold it (2.7, 4.2).
    8. Give it a "when". Add a gate or a cap so in-character states are left alone. For long conversations, consider injecting in the attention output, not the residual stream (3, 4.1, 4.3).
    9. Run the controls: the same center and gain with the matrix removed (2.6), a random subspace with a matched spectrum (COAST), and a cap on the same direction.
    10. Evaluate per turn, with identity probes and persona-replacement attacks, and score coherence separately from the trait (4.2).

    Open cell: a gated conceptor

    A conceptor supplies the shape, and a DSAS-style gate or a cap supplies the "when": (1 − g)h + g·(m + C(h − m)). Nobody has tested it on personas or across turns. Plate 2.8 shows the toy version.

    In character AND NOT broken

    Fit one conceptor on turns where the model stayed in character and one on turns where it broke, then combine them as COAST did for robot success and failure. If breaks look alike across personas, the way failures looked alike across robot tasks, one "not broken" conceptor could serve many personas.

    Compare spectra across models

    A conceptor's list of keep-fractions does not depend on how a model's axes are oriented. Persona conceptors from Llama, Qwen and Gemma can be compared without aligning the models. Rerun persona_acts.py with a 7–8B model to get the data.
