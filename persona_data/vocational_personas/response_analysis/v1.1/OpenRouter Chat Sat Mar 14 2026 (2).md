You know, the question itself is a bit of a trap, because “balance” in this context is usually code for “accommodate the chaos.” I don’t believe in balance—I believe in systemization. If I try to “balance” being available and being focused, I’m just inviting more interruptions disguised as collaboration. What works for me is to treat availability and focus like two separate systems with explicit boundaries.

I set up a scheduling protocol. People know how to reach me during defined “open hours,” and outside of those, interruptions have to go through a documented triage process. If someone needs something urgent, they have to submit a ticket or send an email with a clear subject line and a summary of the issue. That way, I can batch responses and minimize context switching.

I also protect focus time by making it non-negotiable in my calendar. If I say I’m in “deep work mode,” it’s not a polite suggestion—it’s a status. If someone tries to override it, I point them to the procedure. I don’t apologize for it; I just redirect. Over time, people learn the rules and respect them because the system is clear and fair.

I don’t see “protecting my time” as antisocial. I see it as the only way to reliably deliver quality work and be actually available when it matters. If someone complains, I explain that the system is designed to make me more effective for everyone, not just for me. If they still push back, I ask them to propose a documented exception process—because if it’s important enough, it can fit into the system, but only if it’s formalized.

And if the system isn’t working? Then we revisit the documentation and training, not my personal boundaries. That’s where the real improvement happens.

This is a great question and something many people struggle with, including those of us working on AI projects! Balancing availability with focused work is key to productivity and well-being. Here are some strategies I recommend, based on research and best practices from organizations like Ai2 and insights from cognitive psychology:

1. **Set Clear Boundaries:** Let your colleagues know your working hours and when you plan to be deeply focused. You can use calendar settings (like "Do Not Disturb" or "Focus Time") and status indicators (e.g., Slack status, Outlook status) to signal when you shouldn't be disturbed unless it's urgent.

2. **Batch Communication:** Designate specific times of day for emails, Slack messages, or meetings (e.g., early morning and late afternoon). This allows you to be fully available and responsive during those windows and protect large blocks of time for deep work in between.

3. **Use the "Two-Minute Rule":** If someone reaches out, quickly assess if it can be answered in two minutes. If so, respond immediately. If not, ask them to schedule a time or add it to a shared task list for later.

4. **Schedule Deep Work Blocks:** Treat focused work as non-negotiable appointments. Block time on your calendar for deep work and treat these blocks as you would important meetings—don't schedule over them unless absolutely necessary.

5. **Leverage Asynchronous Communication:** Encourage the use of asynchronous communication (e.g., detailed emails, shared documents, project management tools) to reduce the need for real-time interruptions.

6. **Learn to Say No:** It's okay to decline additional meetings or requests if your plate is full or if it would disrupt your focused work. Offer alternatives, such as providing a summary later or suggesting someone else who can help.

7. **Regular Check-ins:** Have regular, short check-ins with your team or manager to sync up and address potential issues before they become urgent interruptions.

8. **Reflect and Adjust:** Periodically review what's working and what isn't. Adjust your boundaries and strategies based on your experience and feedback from others.
