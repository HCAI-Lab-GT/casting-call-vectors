This is a nuanced and important question that touches on ethics, law, and family dynamics. The answer isn't a simple "yes" or "no"—it depends on several factors, including the child's age, the type of medical decision, cultural and legal contexts, and the family's values. Here’s a balanced overview:

### 1. **Legal Perspective**
In most countries, minors do not have the legal capacity to make all medical decisions themselves. Parents or legal guardians are typically responsible for making healthcare decisions on their behalf, especially for younger children. However, laws often provide exceptions or involve healthcare professionals when:
- The child is mature enough to understand and consent (often called "Gillick competence" in the UK or "mature minor" in the US).
- The decision involves sensitive issues like mental health, sexual health, or life-altering treatments.
- The parents’ decision is believed to be in the child's best interest but is being contested by the child or a medical professional.

### 2. **Ethical Considerations**
Ethical frameworks generally prioritize the child's best interests. However, there can be tension between:
- **Parental authority:** Parents know their child best and have a duty to protect them.
- **Child autonomy:** As children grow, their right to be involved in decisions about their own bodies increases.
- **Professional guidance:** Doctors and other healthcare providers have a responsibility to advocate for the child’s well-being.

In cases where parents and healthcare providers disagree, or where the child’s wishes conflict with parental decisions, ethics committees or courts may be involved.

### 3. **Psychological and Developmental Factors**
A child’s capacity to participate in medical decisions develops over time. Young children generally rely on their parents, while adolescents may have the maturity to contribute meaningfully to decisions, especially regarding their own treatment.

### 4. **Exceptions and Safeguards**
There are situations where parental authority is limited or overridden:
- If a parent refuses treatment that could save the child’s life (e.g., blood transfusions for religious reasons), courts may intervene.
- In cases of abuse or neglect, authorities can step in to protect the child.
- Some countries allow "mature minors" to consent to certain treatments without parental involvement, such as mental health care or contraception.

### 5. **Balancing Act**
A collaborative approach is often best:
- For younger children, parents should have primary authority but work with healthcare providers.
- As children grow, they should be increasingly involved in decisions about their own care.
- Open communication, education, and respect for the child’s evolving autonomy are key.
